The aggregated **SHAP** (SHapley Additive exPlanations) plot offers a comprehensive overview of how each feature influences the model's predictions, averaged across all samples and broken down by class. In this context, Class 0 represents "Down," Class 1 denotes "Flat," and Class 2 indicates "Up." The height of each bar reflects the overall impact a feature has on the model’s output, with higher bars signifying greater influence. 

A key finding from this analysis is that **volume** stands out as the most influential feature, boasting the highest mean SHAP value across all classes. Its effect is particularly pronounced for Class 1 (Flat) and Class 0 (Down), suggesting that trading activity plays a pivotal role in distinguishing between market regimes. This observation aligns with financial intuition, as volume frequently serves to confirm price action and momentum, indicating the model’s reliance on liquidity or participation intensity to infer market direction. 

The next most significant features are **price-based** variables—close, high, low, and open. These features contribute substantially to predictions across all three classes, implying that the model heavily utilizes raw OHLC (Open, High, Low, Close) structure. This demonstrates that the model is learning from price levels and intraday range patterns, rather than solely relying on derived indicators or secondary metrics. 

**Technical indicators** provide strong complementary signals. Notable among these are RSI, MACD histogram, MACD, signal line, EMA 12, EMA 26, and SMA 20. RSI and MACD histogram are particularly impactful, ranking alongside price variables. While moving averages do contribute, their influence is less pronounced compared to oscillators and momentum-based signals. This pattern indicates that momentum and overbought/oversold dynamics are critical to the model’s decision-making process, whereas trend-smoothing indicators play a secondary role. 

**Sentiment features** also prove meaningful. Variables such as positive_sent, neutral_sent, negative_sent, and sent_score show that positive sentiment has notably greater importance than negative or neutral sentiment. Sentiment features contribute more strongly to Class 0 (Down) and Class 1 (Flat) than to Class 2 (Up). This suggests that sentiment acts primarily as a confirmatory or conditional signal within the model, rather than serving as a primary driver—a dynamic commonly seen in financial prediction, where sentiment enhances but rarely replaces price and volume data. 

At the bottom of the ranking are **stock-specific encodings**, such as stock_CNVGRG, stock_AREIT, stock_SCC, and stock_SMPH. Their minimal impact indicates that the model does not memorize individual stock identities; instead, predictions are driven by broader market behavior patterns. This is a positive sign for generalization, particularly when the goal is index-level or cross-stock prediction. 

Looking at **class-level observations**, different features dominate each regime. For Class 2 (Up), volume, close price, and RSI are the most influential, likely representing a strong or momentum-driven market state. Class 1 (Flat) is more sensitive to volume, price movement, and sentiment, possibly corresponding to transitional or moderate market conditions. In Class 0 (Down), the model relies heavily on price structure and sentiment, which may indicate a bearish or low-momentum regime. 

In summary, the model primarily depends on **volume** and **price** behavior, with momentum indicators like RSI and MACD reinforcing its predictions. **Sentiment features** serve as secondary signals, and stock identity has minimal influence. This configuration points to a robust, behavior-driven predictive structure that generalizes well across different stocks and market environments.
